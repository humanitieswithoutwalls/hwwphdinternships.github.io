# **Onboarding and Orientation**

The processes for matching and placing students will vary across institutions and be shaped by a variety of factors, including but not limited to:
* Exisitng infrastructure for service learning;
* Relationships with community organizations and their desired level of involvement in the application/selection process;
* Needs identified by the organizations an intern can fulfill

Some of the R&R in Action programs at HWW consortial partner institutions identified organziations before recruiting applications from students and shaped the selection criteria around the needs the organization specified. In some cases, the organziations interviewed applicants as part of the placement process. 

The HWW Summer Bridge Program selected students first, then their information is immediately shared with our partners at WeCU, who then sent the students a project matching survey where students were asked to rank causes and issues they are most interested in. WeCU used the survey information to identify local organizations and began contacting them to put the internship in place. The decision to structure placement in this way is based on the infrastructure established by WeCU in working with service learning students and their longstanding relationships with local community organizations. See the Placement page for a more detailed overview of this process.

When the interns begin working with their partner organizations, the organization will orient them during their first week. Prior to that, in the late spring semester, students should also attend a program orientation with the coordinator to cover the methods and ethos of the program, logistics and schedule, meet their cohort members, and address any questions or issues that need to be resolved before the start of the program. As in the info session and application, the orientation session will re-emphasize to the students that the needs of their community partners are of paramount importance and this type of work requires them to position themselves differently than other sorts of academic work. Being flexible and adaptable to their community partner's needs may require them to shift priorities in ways traditional academic contexts would not. For example, the Summer Bridge Program requires students to be physically present in Champaign-Urbana for the full eight weeks of the program in order to work in-person with their partner organizations. While the occassional remote work day may be necessary for circumstantial reasons, in-person work is the default. This means that things like travel for conferences or other events are not permitted. Because the program is only 8 weeks, students are expected to commit to the organization's needs and be fully present for the duration of the summer. While such a short program does not counter the drop-in-drop-out culture that is so often prevelent in service learning and community organization relationships, the intensive nature of the program aims to cultivate deep engagement during the student's temporary placement. The primary reasoning behind a policy such as this is to make sure having an intern does place a burden on the community organization and that they are not having to work to accommodate the needs of the student, but are instead able to quickly orient the student and immerse them in areas of need.
